&emsp; &emsp; *Loves of a Blonde* (1965), *Daisies* (1966), and *Valerie and Her Week of Wonders* (1970), all pivotal films in the Czech new wave, are related in their use of visual rhymes and symbolism in describing the female experience in a burgeoning Czech society, reflecting the pressure of societal expectations, female identity, and the complexity of love and sexuality. By employing recurring symbols like rings and ties in *Loves of a Blonde*, butterflies and phallic objects in *Daisies,* and blood, flowers, and religious iconography in *Valerie and Her Week of Wonders*, these films explore themes of sexual awakening, female objectification, and societal hypocrisy. Through the repetition of these visual resemblances, the films illustrate how patriarchal norms manifest in both intimate and institutional settings. Ultimately, these recurring images do not just highlight the female struggle for autonomy but also expose the broader mechanisms of power that sustain male dominance in Czechoslovak society.

&emsp; &emsp; Miloš Forman’s *Loves of a Blonde* uses visual motifs like rings and ties to explore the tension between romantic ideals and the harsh realities of female objectification in a transitioning Czechoslovakia. Forman observes such strong themes through a single girl, Andula, and her efforts to find love in her contemporary society. The film pays homage to the Italian Neorealist style, taking the audience through the quotidian experiences of Andula and her efforts to find a man who treats her like a person, and not just a sexual object. Visual resemblances and symbolism are prevalent throughout the film, taking figures through the promises that come with rings and ties. In the film, rings represent a man’s promise to a woman, often a false one, and ties represent a woman's promise to a man, only to find out his true intentions.

&emsp; &emsp; The ring in *Loves of a Blonde* serves as a recurring symbol of commitment, juxtaposed against the reality of male deception and infidelity. It first appears when Andula plays with one given to her by her then fiancé, Tonda, representing her hope for an eternal bond. However, the ring’s symbolic promise quickly unravels in the ballroom scene, where an older married soldier, under great peer pressure, takes off his ring before attempting to woo Andula and her friends. When the ring slips from his hand, it rolls toward a group of women he upset earlier, visually emphasizing how easily men discard and lose such commitments. This moment strongly signals the insignificance of a ring to men in this society and foreshadows the consequences of infidelity, hinting at the soldier’s wife’s potential reaction through the three disapproving women. The motif then reappears in Andula’s first encounter with Mila, where they lay together in bed, sharing that both of their rings are from their mothers. Their shared laughter in the moment signifies an unspoken acknowledgment of the stories as false. It is an indication of an awareness from both sides of infidelity as a fact of Czech life. Despite this awareness, however, Andula continues to take rings and offer ties in hopes of genuine connection, only to find them dismissed by men more interested in fleeting encounters (Hames, 115). Through the usage of the ring as a visual resemblance, the film critiques the trivialization of female fidelity in a patriarchal society that prioritizes male autonomy.

&emsp; &emsp; As the film continues, the tie as a visual resemblance emerges when Andula buys one for her ex-boyfriend Tonda, hoping that a faithful promise will come with it. The tie first appears wrapped around a tree in the forest, alluding to the aforementioned opening scene of Andula playing with a ring. Through both instances, a visual parallel is created, likening both the tie and the ring to serve as torus tokens that reflect Andula’s hope for a lasting bond, despite the patriarchal constraints that ultimately favor male desire over genuine reciprocity. While waiting for Tonda, the Forest Ranger instead shows up and tells Andula of geese mating forever, an untrue anecdote but a hopeful one nonetheless. Andula, persisting in her illusions, takes the goose story as a hopeful symbol for her future, that Tonda might be that “goose” who will stay with her forever. The audience, at very much the same time, knows that geese, in reality, do not mate forever, and even Andula concludes that it is clear that the forest ranger talking to her was just another of many men who see her as a sexual entity and not a human being.

&emsp; &emsp; The tie is then later visually rhymed with this scene when Andula goes to Mila’s apartment, the *coup de grâce* in her relationship with Mila one might say, displaying that the tie is a symbol of Andula’s love for each new man in her life. In the small suitcase she brings to his apartment, hopeful of reuniting with the boy who she “trusted more than anyone,” lays that tie. She hopes to give this tie to Mila, perhaps to, in her own way, propose to him, to tie it around his neck, and lock in their relationship.  The tie serves as a representation of what Andula thinks a man would want from a woman, a symbol of their connection, a gift that he can wear every day and know the meaning behind it. It is her version of the ring. Yet, what the audience knows all too well, and what Andula comes to find out, is that Mila doesn’t want that tie, and even if he took it, it would be meaningless to him. Mila comes back to find Andula in his home, right after having been with another woman moments before. The tie and rings both serve as promises, but in the end, they are promises that are only ever made on Andula’s side in her conscious blindness of the situations she is in. She chooses hope and love every time, while the man chooses greed and selfishness. This reflects the patriarchal double standard that encourages women to be monogamous and chaste, while men are free to act without constraint, pursuing their desires without consequence. The tie and ring’s symbolism in conjunction thus underscores how societal norms skew romantic dynamics, how what the woman gives to the man in a patriarchal society is much more important, yet male autonomy is favored over female fidelity.

&emsp; &emsp; Věra Chytilová’s *Daisies* contends with a patriarchal-dominated communist society in Czechoslovakia, and by focusing on two girls' absurd interactions with the world around them, lacking spatial or causal continuity, the film plays out more like an essay than it does a typically constructed contiguous film. Although entirely stylistically different from *Loves of a Blonde*, Chytilová, like the other filmmakers of her time, incorporates numerous visual resemblances—with special emphasis on phallic objects as a representation of patriarchal society and butterflies as a metaphor for male objectification of women—to hint to her audience the true female-empowering meaning of her film, one that escaped censorship from the male dominance of the time.

&emsp; &emsp; One of the first visual symbols in the film is the rhyme between butterflies and women, the rapid repetition of such reveals patriarchal attitudes toward female sexuality and anatomy. In a scene where Jarmila, one of the main characters, is semi-nude in a bedroom with a man named Romeo, the audience gets to experience firsthand the patriarchal undertones engraved into the everyday Czech man. Romeo, an avid butterfly collector, watches jittery as Jarmila stands in the room, covering her genitalia with frames of his precious butterflies. It is clear to the audience how fixated Romeo is on attempting to engage in sexual intercourse with Jarmila. He appears to be going simply crazy at the fact that she won’t let him, a direct effect of the strong sexually charged urges that he is “expected” to release in a patriarchal society. As a man he should be able to have his way with women in this patriarchal view, and a woman not allowing him to do so only pushes him to want it more. Furthermore, as a butterfly collector, Romeo’s goal is to continuously capture any butterfly he can find, especially pining for the rare ones. This is a clear metaphor for the way women were treated by men at the time, with the butterfly/woman visual rhyme highlighting the objectification at play. The single-frame butterfly animations that appear earlier in the sequence, and which frequently interrupt the action, reinforce this connection by equating Romeo’s obsession with butterflies to his views of women as objects to be captured and displayed.

&emsp; &emsp; Building on the butterfly/woman visual rhyme established in the previous scene, Chytilová introduces the needle pinned to the butterfly as the first of many phallic objects that recur throughout the film, reinforcing the themes of male dominance and female objectification. The butterfly’s shape roughly resembles that of the female body, and each of those that Romeo has collected is pinned right in the center. This pinned needle, used to hold butterflies in their containers, parallels the male genitalia in its symbolic act of penetrating and immobilizing the female body. Romeo itches to add Jarmila to his vast collection of women, just as he would a beautiful butterfly. Later, as Jarmila places the butterfly frames over her genitalia, Chytilová through careful staging likens that which Romeo wants most to collect (Jarmila’s sex) to what he collects professionally (the butterflies in the frame), rooting his desire in sexual conquest, as mirrored by his professional pursuit. Just as men hunt beautiful animals, they similarly pursue women, seeking possession and control rather than genuine human connection.

&emsp; &emsp; This phallic needle to pin the butterflies then rhymes further on with the numerous phallic objects cut up and eaten in the following scene. Jarmila and Ježinka are shown eating pickles and sausages, slicing them up brutally before eating them. Rather than simply picking pickles from a large jar at their bedside, these two women are defining their autonomy by destroying male power through the ingestion and seizure of their manhood (the phallic pickles). The jar of pickles can be likened to a collection, such as Romeo’s, flipping the typical patriarchal standard of men collecting women to women destroying men. The multiplicity of phallic foods in this scene emphasizes that the aggression is directed not solely at Romeo, but at the patriarchy and the power it gives men in general. By chopping up the food, the women are metaphorically castrating their oppressors, stripping them of their manhood, taking down the patriarchy, and defining their autonomy (Eagle 232). The symbolic cannibalism represents the women using their femininity to disrupt the patriarchal views of their country at the time. In many cultures, cannibalism is used for the ritual function of consuming one’s enemy to defeat their power and suppression. It is thus evident that Chytilová is creating a direct tie between this scene and the one with Jarmila and Romeo, essentially suggesting that a man who seeks to exploit a woman sexually deserves to be dismembered in turn.

&emsp; &emsp; The phallic imagery continues beyond this scene, reinforcing the film’s critique of patriarchal control through sexual metaphors. For example, when Jarmila and Ježinka climb atop the chandelier in the dining room, it mirrors a phallic ascent, symbolizing their brief rise in power over male domination. However, the subsequent fall and crashing of the chandelier serves as a punishment for their defiance, illustrating how patriarchal forces continue to retaliate against female rebellion. Similarly, the long oars that are supposedly “saving them” from drowning are in reality plunging them into the river, resembling medieval poles used for witch dunking, another historical punishment for defiant women. Chytilová uses these sequences to demonstrate that while women may temporarily seize power, the patriarchal system in its contemporary state is relentless in its efforts to reassert control. The final epigraph, along with these final moments of punishment by phallic objects, only certifies the emphasis on the cyclical nature of male dominance furthermore, suggesting that any disruption of patriarchal order is met with violent retribution, and reaffirming the deep-seated nature of gender oppression in Czech society.

&emsp; &emsp; In *Valerie and Her Week of Wonders*, visual rhymes seem to bind the dreamlike and realistic landscapes, making it nearly impossible for the audience to determine which is which. This film, similar in some ways to *Daisies*, employs a vastly different visual style compared to the realism of *Loves of a Blonde*, but with a similar sentiment focused on the plight of maturing as a woman. Visual resemblances recur often: blood represents sexual awakening and loss of innocence; flowers symbolize purity and virginity; and religious icons draw attention to the deception of religious authority. Through surrealist imagery and semi-discontinuous storytelling, the film conveys Valerie’s struggle to maintain innocence in a society rife with sin, as well as her confrontation with patriarchal expectations tied to religion and sexuality. Jaromil Jireš orchestrates the repetition of these symbols to create visual rhymes that reinforce the narrative’s central themes. Imagery is essential, and each scene’s blocking and composition contribute to the broader message, showing how these elements intertwine to depict Valerie’s coming-of-age. Ultimately, the film’s visual complexity through its resemblances invites the viewer to consider how patriarchal forces shape female identity and the struggle for autonomy.

&emsp; &emsp; Valerie getting her first period is a catalyst for the fantasy-filled journey she is thrown into, with flowers serving as a recurring symbol that marks her transition into womanhood. Early on, Valerie is seen walking through her town when below her the camera shows us daisies becoming bloodied. The daisies stand as a symbol of youth and innocence, a beautiful flower colored purely white, resembling a pristine being. As the blood falls upon the daisy, it becomes tainted, symbolizing Valerie’s loss of innocence as she crosses into adulthood. The transformation foreshadows the patriarchal aggressions, restrictions, sexual objectification, and double standards that Valerie will soon encounter. Furthermore, often a woman’s virginity is likened to a flower, and the taking of such is referred to as “deflowering,” foreshadowing that Valerie’s virginity is in peril and that throughout the film it will be in danger.

&emsp; &emsp; Flowers continue to show up throughout the film, especially carried by a young girl dressed in white who emerges just before or after Valerie is in peril. Her presence before danger seems to warn of the impending threat to Valerie’s innocence, such as in the scene in the church as Tchoř preaches, reminding the audience of the pure being that she currently is. Her appearance after such moments represents the patriarchal expectation of Valerie to remain as pure as ever, even with all external efforts to taint her. The girl stands as a metaphorical benchmark that the patriarchal Czech society expects Valerie to reflect, even while men are constantly trying to take advantage of her. As a symbol of chastity and societal expectations, the flower girl represents the unattainable ideal of purity that is continually imposed upon Valerie, reinforcing the persistent pressures of patriarchal norms.

&emsp; &emsp; Shortly after, Valerie reveals to her grandmother that she is no longer just a normal child, and her grandmother tells her to take off her earrings immediately. As she continues playing with a single one, her grandmother scolds her, a seemingly trivial act, but one that metaphorically represents sexual discovery. Rather than playing with simply an earring, the film is alluding toward Valerie playing with her genitalia, a part of her that between the transition from child to adult no longer becomes just a part of her anatomy, but a sexualized part of her being. Orlik, perhaps her brother, perhaps her boyfriend, or perhaps even just a stranger in love with her, steals away her earrings before the daisy becomes bloodied, knowing that the earrings are desired by Tchoř for nefarious purposes (Owen 203). Here Tchoř embodies the old patriarchal ways of Czechoslovakia, seeking to control Valerie’s sexuality through deceit and manipulation. In contrast, Orlik, through his attempts to prevent this evil, represents a potential shift in male youth behavior— a new, protective presence that aims to shield rather than exploit women. Orlik’s act of returning the earrings, coveted by the Constable, signals him as a rebel against the institutional patriarchal ownership of women. His actions portray a new utopian version of a man, focused on a balanced and respectful relationship rather than dominance. The earrings carry with them Valerie’s virginity, and the interactions that men have with them throughout the film define the way male society views her.  They are a metaphor for what the typical man in Valerie’s life will forever want from her in the future: her virginity, her body, and her sex.

&emsp; &emsp; In *Valerie*, sexual symbolism is not the only form present, the constant imagery of vampires and religion through Tchoř’s presence in the film serve to represent the Church’s disguised participation in the sexual exploitation of women and its maintenance of the double standard. Tchoř, masquerading as the Bishop, a highly respected man in his religious sect, uses his authority to attempt to corrupt Valerie. While he preaches to the young virgins to stay pure, he at the same time attempts to defile Valerie and take advantage of her innocence, embodying the hypocrisy of religious figures who exploit their power for sexual gain. Tchoř is not alone in this behavior, as demonstrated by Father Graziano’s attempt to sexually assault Valerie in her bedroom after a night of drinking and telling her how good of a friend he was to her father. This displays the hypocrisy in some of what religious figures preach, while also drawing attention to the unpredictability and viciousness of men when under sexual influence in the patriarchy. Tchoř’s two-facedness and constant shift between forms create a rhyming device that serves to represent the hidden mal-intentioned nature of men present in a young woman coming-of-age's life. Valerie knows this man by many names and faces by the end of the film, and his name itself (Tchoř means weasel) is a double-entendre, serving as his literal name and also as a description of his character. He is a man who is at one point a disgusting predator preying upon Valerie at her most vulnerable and at another moment her father. Tchoř’s multiple forms directly correlate to allowing Valerie to understand the multiple forms that patriarchal power takes in her society.

&emsp; &emsp; In Valerie’s house, there are two wooden paintings, one of her father and one of her mother, both of which resemble classical wood paintings of what might be Jesus and the Virgin Mary. These visual symbols serve as a way to represent the way children praise and look up to their parents, and also their lack of questioning authority. Valerie never really questions exactly what happened to her parents, just accepts that they are gone. Her father, depicted in this semi-Jesus-like painting, later is revealed to be an alternate face of Tchoř, displaying furthermore the two-facedness of religion, and the hypocrisy that comes with it. While his face stays fairly stagnant throughout the film as the spitting image of Nosferatu, in the final third of the film his face often oscillates between grotesque vampire and human father, a metaphor that in the patriarchy any man can be the enemy.

&emsp; &emsp; Although each is incredibly different in its style and content, thematically these films converge to portray the evolving female image in light of a patriarchal society. Rather than depicting women simply as victims or subjects of their male counterparts, these films illustrate their active navigation of, resistance to, and battle against male-dominated norms. In *Loves of a Blonde*, Andula’s pursuit of love and connection is framed by patriarchal double standards that undermine her efforts, as her genuine desire for commitment is met with male selfishness and opportunism. Despite the havoc wreaked by male sexual conquest, Andula remains resilient, symbolizing women’s ongoing struggle to find meaning and intimacy within oppressive structures. In *Daisies*, the women’s absurd behavior and sexual provocations serve as a critique of patriarchy, using playful transgression to unmask and mock male desires. This subversive approach highlights their rebellion against male authority rather than being subjugated as objects who exercise seduction. Lastly, in *Valerie and Her Week of Wonders*, the audience witnesses a young woman coming of age among the predatory behavior of men and religious figures in their obsession with virginity and a “pure” woman. Ultimately, each film critiques the male-dominated world and seeks to challenge the limits on women’s roles in society. Whether through overt defiance or symbolic resistance, the women in these films strive for autonomy and confront patriarchal forces head-on. By doing so, they offer a warning to society about the consequences of continuing to oppress women, while also portraying a hopeful vision of female agency. Collectively, these works highlight the anti-patriarchal themes that define the Czech New Wave, as each film envisions a future where women challenge societal norms and redefine their roles on their own terms.
